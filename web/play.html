You start walking on the rock: gravity pulls you down, the ground holds you up, and a stone face stops you dead. All three come from sampling the field under two spheres rather than from a collision mesh, because a collider built from this terrain is stale the moment you carve — which is every frame you hold the button. V jumps about a unit, enough to climb out of a brush. F switches back to the fly mode this demo used to open in, where gravity is off and Q and E go straight up and down.

Hold the left button to carve, the right to fill. The translucent sphere on the rock under the crosshair is the brush that a click would push — orange to carve, cyan to fill — so what you see is what you get, at the surface rather than at a fixed distance in front of your eye. The wheel or [ ] resizes it, Z undoes one brush, X clears the log, C outlines the chunks the last edit re-meshed.

Holding a button carves at a fixed 20 edits a second rather than one per frame, and the chunks that fall out of it are re-meshed under a 4 ms per frame budget — nearest to the camera first, with whatever does not fit carried into the next frame. That is the difference between a long stroke that holds its frame time and one that collapses: every sample of every chunk still evaluates every brush in the log, so the only two things that can save you are growing the log more slowly and refusing to pay for all of it at once.

1–8 swap the mesher and re-mesh all 256 chunks with it — Marching Cubes, the same with the asymptotic decider, Marching Tetrahedra, Surface Nets, Dual Contouring, Manifold Dual Contouring, Greedy Quads, and on 8 Marching Cubes again but on the GPU, as a compute shader through isomesh-gpu. The rendering was always on the GPU; what moves on 8 is the extraction — the field is uploaded, the cells are counted and prefix-scanned and the triangles emitted on the device, and the vertices come back a frame later rather than in the call that asked for them. The HUD prints what each choice cost, which is the only honest way to show that Marching Tetrahedra is three times the triangles. A dimmed "Loading… please wait." panel covers the screen while a switch's 256-chunk backlog drains, because that backlog now drains across frames instead of blocking one.

The mesh is rebuilt while you hold the mouse down, and the two numbers on screen are the ones that matter under load: chunks touched per edit, and E1 — the fraction of the brush's own bounding box that actually changed. M-33 measured 15–36% offline; this is it live.

H hides the whole panel and leaves a single [H] HUD line behind, so you can look at the rock without reading numbers over it and still find your way back.

The rock is cracked concrete, textured by the fragment shader rather than by the mesh: an isosurface has no natural parameterisation, so the material samples all three world planes and interpolates between them by the normal. Carve a fresh hole and the new interior surface is textured continuously with the ground around it, across the chunk boundary the brush straddled.

This is the demo that needs IsomeshPlugin's streaming, and until ✗44 that plugin panicked on its first chunk in any browser.

It runs at its committed defaults here. The example reads ISOMESH_* environment variables to override the stream radius and the frame budget, and a browser has no environment — so every one of them takes the documented default rather than something a URL chose.

One thing to expect if you open the console on this one: streaming churns meshes continuously, and Bevy 0.19's bevy_render slab allocator logs "Use-after-free: attempted to copy element data for an unallocated key" once per affected mesh — 3,186 times in a 45-second run, measured on the WebGL2 backend this page served before the nine demos moved to WebGPU, so read the count as the shape of the thing and not as today's figure. The demo streams and renders correctly through all of it (411 chunks resident, 229,580 triangles). It is upstream of this repository and it is not suppressed here, because a filter that hides a renderer's errors would also hide the next real one.
